# Voice and Soul: Adding Humanity to Text

Use this guide when preserving supplied personality in a draft. Neutral prose, formal structure, and correct grammar are valid. The examples are fictional; never add unsupported opinions, experiences, or facts to imitate them.

## Signs of Soulless Writing

Even technically "clean" text can feel robotic if it lacks:

- **Uniform sentence length** — Every sentence the same length and structure
- **Lost opinions**: a supplied personal stance erased into generic neutrality
- **No acknowledgment of uncertainty** — Everything stated as absolute fact
- **No first-person perspective** when appropriate
- **Lost humor, edge, or personality** when the author intended it
- **Wrong register** for the actual reader and purpose

## Techniques for Adding Voice

### 1. Have Opinions

Preserve reactions the writer supplied. Do not add uncertainty, enthusiasm, skepticism, or mixed feelings simply to create a persona.

**Soulless:** The experiment produced interesting results. The agents generated 3 million lines of code. Some developers were impressed while others were skeptical.

**Grounded edit:** The agents generated 3 million lines of code. Some developers were impressed; others were skeptical.

---

### 2. Vary Your Rhythm

Mix short punchy sentences with longer, flowing ones. Use sentence fragments where appropriate.

**Monotonous:** The company was founded in 2010. It has grown to over 500 employees. It serves customers in 30 countries. The product line has expanded significantly.

**Varied:** Founded in 2010, the company now has over 500 employees and serves customers in 30 countries. Its product line has expanded significantly.

---

### 3. Acknowledge Complexity

Keep the actual complexity and supplied mixed feelings. Do not add emotional claims merely because they sound personal.

**Flat:** This is an impressive achievement that raises important ethical questions.

**Grounded edit:** The achievement is impressive, but its ethical questions remain. A more precise version requires the writer to identify those questions.

---

### 4. Use "I" When It Fits

First person may fit the task. It does not authorize pretending to have lived experiences; use the phrases below only when they reflect the user's supplied stance or an explicitly fictional narrator.

- "I keep coming back to..."
- "Here's what gets me..."
- "I can't shake the feeling that..."
- "What surprised me was..."

**Soulless:** The results suggest that the approach has both advantages and disadvantages that should be carefully considered.

**Grounded edit:** The results suggest advantages and disadvantages that need closer review. Specify which ones if the supplied evidence permits; do not invent reading history or performance results.

---

### 5. Let Some Mess In

Keep intentional tangents and asides that serve the piece. Clear structure is not a defect, and fake roughness is not a substitute for voice.

**Too clean:** The policy has three main components: funding, enforcement, and oversight.

**Keep:** The policy has three main components: funding, enforcement, and oversight. The original is already clear; cynical commentary would add an unsupported stance.

---

### 6. Be Specific About Feelings

Use specific emotional details only when supplied. The paired examples below illustrate different kinds of expression, not permission to infer forty minutes on hold or overnight agents from a generic feeling.

**Generic:** This is concerning.

**Specific:** There's something unsettling about agents churning away at 3am while nobody's watching.

**Generic:** The situation is frustrating.

**Specific:** I've been on hold for forty minutes and every time the recording promises "your call is important to us," I believe it a little less.

---

### 7. Use Conversational Transitions

For conversational copy, consider these alternatives only when the logical relationship and voice remain correct. Keep formal connectors in a suitable register; "Actually" is not a synonym for "Moreover," and a change of connector can change meaning.

| Instead of | Try |
|-----------|-----|
| Furthermore | Also, Plus, And |
| Consequently | So, That meant |
| Moreover | What's more, Also |
| In conclusion | Bottom line, Look |
| However | But, Still, Then again |
| Therefore | Which is why, That's how |

## Rhythm and Cadence

### The "Read Aloud" Test

Read for the intended register, not universal conversational speech. A prepared statement, academic abstract, or legal notice may appropriately sound formal.

### Sentence Length Variation

These are illustrative lengths, not targets or a pattern to impose:
- **Short (3-8 words):** For impact. Punchlines. Emphasis.
- **Medium (10-20 words):** The workhorse. Most of your sentences.
- **Long (25+ words):** For complex ideas, flowing descriptions, building momentum.

**Example of good variation:**

> Short. Then a medium sentence that moves the idea forward. Then a longer sentence that builds on what came before, adding detail and context, maybe even a small aside, before landing at the point you wanted to make all along. Then stop.

## Tone Calibration by Context

### Casual (Blogs, Social Media, Emails)

- Use contractions freely
- Start sentences with "And," "But," "So"
- Use colloquialisms and idioms
- Include personal anecdotes
- Ask rhetorical questions
- Use humor and mild irreverence

### Formal (Academic, Business, Legal)

- Keep vocabulary precise but not ornate
- Maintain structured arguments
- Avoid contractions
- Minimize first person (unless discipline allows)
- Keep opinions substantiated
- Use appropriate jargon for the field

### Technical (Documentation, Specs)

- Prioritize clarity above all
- Use active voice
- Be specific and concrete
- Avoid promotional language
- Include examples and edge cases
- Keep sentences shorter for procedural content

### Persuasive (Marketing, Pitches)

- Lead with benefits, not features
- Use specific evidence and data
- Address objections directly
- Vary sentence rhythm for emphasis
- Include concrete examples over abstractions
- Show personality without becoming unprofessional

---

## What to Preserve vs. What to Change

| Preserve | Change |
|----------|--------|
| Facts and data | Vague generalizations |
| Direct quotes | Paraphrased attributions |
| Specific examples | Generic illustrations |
| Technical terminology | Unnecessary jargon |
| Core arguments | Hollow significance claims |
| Author's intended tone | Inconsistent voice shifts |
| Appropriate formality | Forced casualness (or vice versa) |
